Phase 0 report · batching potential · Base · 10 days of data (Jul 25 – Aug 3)

How much better would trades settle if we batched them?

We took every solver trade on Base for 10 days — 868,783 of them — and asked: how often could two opposite trades in the same block have been matched directly, and how much better would each trader have done? Each trade also carries Fynd's quote, so we can separate what batching adds from what Fynd already delivers. All weekly dollar figures are normalized per 7 days.

Summary

  1. Batching one block at a time adds almost nothing on top of Fynd: 0.02 bps per trade on average — because 99.4% of trade volume has no opposite trade in its block to match directly — and even counting shared-route matching at its theoretical ceiling, 98.3% cannot be helped at all. Fynd's quotes are already close to the best achievable price, and for the vast majority of trades a single-block batch has literally nothing to do. Even the most generous ceiling — every matching mechanism, optimistic pricing — is under 1 bps per trade. For scale: users typically set 100 bps of slippage tolerance.
  2. Tokens Fynd can't route offer almost no real matching either: ~$150/wk. This flow (37% of all trades, more on weekends) is mostly people buying brand-new tokens — everyone trades in the same direction at once, so opposite trades rarely exist in the same block. What looks like opposite flow there is almost entirely bots trading with themselves (details in the verification section).
  3. Waiting longer changes the picture. Collect trades for 1 minute and 11.6% of volume finds a match (0.53 bps per trade); at 5 minutes, 21.2% (1.22 bps). Batching value on Base comes from waiting, not from the batch mechanism itself.
  4. One mechanism is still unmeasured: matching through shared routes. Two orders that don't oppose each other directly can still partially cancel out if the batch routes them through a common token (example below). This is 66% of the theoretical matching volume within one block, and simulation is the only way to price it. That is the one concrete question left for the batch-solver simulation phase.
  5. The biggest finding is not about batching: 25% of all Base solver trades are in tokens whose liquidity sits in Uniswap v4 pools with custom hooks (creator coins), which our data feed doesn't index. Supporting those pools would grow Fynd's reachable market by a quarter — worth pursuing regardless of any batching decision.

The gain per trade: prize × odds

When two opposite trades match, both sides win a little: the seller gets more, the buyer pays less. To read the numbers correctly you need exactly two of them, and their product:

The prize — how much better a trade does if it finds an opposite partner in its window.
The odds — what share of trade volume finds a partner at all. Within one block, 99.4% of volume has no direct opposite; even counting matching through shared routes at its theoretical ceiling, 98.3% cannot be helped by any batching mechanism.
Prize × odds = the average gain per trade — what the product actually delivers.
Single block: 3.9 bps prize × 0.6% odds ≈ 0.02 bps average. A lottery with a modest prize and very long odds.

The prize can be measured against two baselines: against Fynd's quote (what batching adds on top of what we already ship — the number that matters), or against the price users actually got (which also includes the improvement Fynd alone would deliver — shown in grey).

How the headline number is measured

The baseline is real, per trade: as the trades happened, our monitor solved each one with Fynd at the pre-trade market state and stored the quote. Per block, where a pair traded in both directions, we take each direction's volume-weighted Fynd price; the gap between them is the room left inside Fynd's own spread — the only thing crossing adds that Fynd doesn't already deliver. Matched amount = the smaller side, in exact token units; gain = matched × gap, counted only when positive (a negative gap means crossing helps no one); dollars via Fynd's own price data. Summed over all blocks: ≈ $330/week across ~$164M/week of quoted volume = 0.02 bps. The only modeled step is the dollar conversion — the rest is arithmetic on recorded quotes.

Read down the columns and the whole story is visible: the prize barely moves with the window (3.9 → 6.1 bps); the odds improve 35-fold (0.6% → 21.2%). Waiting doesn't make individual matches much more valuable — it makes matches exist. That is why batching value on Base comes from the waiting window, not from the matching mechanism itself. And even the prize is small next to the 100 bps of slippage users routinely accept.

The tokens Fynd can't route

37% of trades are in thousands of tokens with no reachable liquidity in our data feed. Direct matching would be the one thing we could offer there — it needs no liquidity at all, just two opposite trades. In practice there is very little to match: ~$150/week of real matching value at one block (~$61k/wk of matched volume at ~25 bps), and a far lower match rate (0.015%) than in normal tokens. New-token trading is one-directional — everyone buys at the same time — so opposite trades in the same block are rare.

Wash exclusion (verified). One pair of these tokens trades tens of millions per week against itself in perfectly opposite same-block flows — it would have added $101M of fake "matched" volume over the 10 days. We looked up who sent the 200 transactions behind its biggest matches: just 10 addresses, nine of them sending 17–29 transactions each. A bot cluster trading with itself, not real user demand. Its matches are excluded from every number in this report.

What this unreachable flow actually is

  1. Creator-coin pools (67% of these trades — 25% of ALL Base solver trades). Zora-style creator and launchpad tokens live in Uniswap v4 pools with custom "hooks" (plug-in contracts that change how the pool prices trades). Our data feed indexes only plain v4 pools, so ~218k trades across 1,741 tokens were invisible to Fynd in these 10 days even though their liquidity is on-chain. This is fixable — and it is the single biggest way to grow the solver's reachable market that this analysis found.
  2. Market-maker-quoted flow (at least 5% of Base aggregator volume). Some tokens trade only against professional market makers who quote prices privately per request — there is no on-chain pool to index, ever. No data-feed expansion reaches this; only direct matching or a market-maker integration would.
  3. Brand-new micro tokens. Most of the unreachable tokens have only a handful of trades — freshly launched, pools too small to index.

The one thing we couldn't measure: matching through shared routes

Two orders don't need to be exact opposites to help each other. Say one user swaps USDC→WETH, and another swaps PEPE→USDC where the PEPE/USDC pool is bad but PEPE/WETH is deep. A batch can route the second order as PEPE→WETH→USDC — and now its WETH→USDC leg is the exact opposite of the first order. Those legs cancel directly; only PEPE→WETH touches a pool, and it's the good pool. Both users win. Only a batch that settles everyone at the same prices can do this.

We can measure the volume of this precisely — it is the largest matching mechanism within a single block — but its value depends on which pools the rerouting avoids, and that requires simulating the batch solver against real pool state. A rough estimate says up to ~$8k/wk within one block, but treat that as a ceiling, not a measurement. This is the concrete question the simulation phase answers. (Cycles, by contrast, are measurable and negligible — they come free with a batch but never justify one.)

How we measured, and what to keep in mind

Input: our own decoder's record of every solver trade on Base for 10 days, each with the price the user actually got and the price Fynd would have quoted at that moment. Trades are cleaned first: multi-step routes collapsed to what the user actually swapped, venue fees removed, ETH and WETH treated as the same asset, obviously mis-sized records excluded (2,855 trades / $30.3M — a known decoder issue with arbitrage trades, fix under way), and the verified bot pair excluded from matching. Matching is computed in exact token amounts; a match only counts as a gain when both sides genuinely improve. Full method with worked examples: methodology page.

  • Weekly figures are normalized from the 10-day span; yearly figures are the week × 52. We checked against five weeks of an independent dataset: the period's match rate is typical (four of five weeks sit at 0.29–0.39%; one volatile week doubled that — turbulent markets briefly create more matching). The 7-day and 10-day runs agree on every headline ratio.
  • The numbers lean conservative: the period predates several coverage additions (one ~$13M/wk trading venue among them), and gas savings from shared settlement are not counted.
  • All "possible matching" figures assume everyone participates and no one's price limit gets in the way — a ceiling by construction. The simulation phase measures what's actually achievable.

What we recommend next

  1. Run the batch-solver simulation, one block at a time, to answer one question: how much of the "shared routes" matching (the unmeasured 66%) turns into real gains? Everything else within one block is now measured at fractions of a basis point. The simulation infrastructure is built and compiling.
  2. Separately from batching: index the creator-coin pools. A quarter of all Base solver trades are currently invisible to Fynd. That's the largest growth lever this analysis found, and it pays off with or without batching.
  3. Keep extending the data window (collection began Jul 25; a full month completes ~Aug 24) and re-run after the decoder fix and coverage additions deploy — minutes of work, same script, but segment the series at the deploy date.
  4. Longer batching windows stay out of scope for now — the table above is the ready-made cost-benefit input if that ever changes.

Pre-analysis proposal · Base · 2026-07-31

How much coincidence of wants is inside a Base block?

Before simulating APEX, measure the one thing only a batcher can capture: opposite trades in the same block that could cross without touching a pool. Methodology, three worked real-block examples, and the scan plan — for greenlight.

Why measure this first

An in-sequencer batcher sees the same pools, the same tokens, and the same orders as Fynd does per-order. Its only structural edge is internalization: when user A sells what user B is buying in the same block, the batch can cross them at one clearing price and skip the AMM round trip — its fee, its price impact, and one leg of gas. Everything else APEX might win on, Fynd already delivers.

So the coincidence-of-wants density of real Base blocks is the ceiling on batching's unique value. If the ceiling is low, the APEX simulation is measuring noise; if high, this same data tells us where (pairs, venues, block windows) the value concentrates. The day-0 numbers above say: thin at 2-second granularity (0.4–2% of volume), so the batching-window sweep is a first-class dimension, not a sensitivity check.

Data & the two traps the examples exposed

Source: base.dex.aggregator_trades (Allium) — solver/venue-routed flow, one row per decoded trade, with block_number, token amounts, and USD values. No chain access needed; the whole scan is SQL + local compute. This is the same trade universe hindsight decodes and the APEX replay would batch, so the numbers compose.

Testing the naive query on real blocks immediately surfaced two traps that define the methodology:

Trap 1 — multi-hop rows fabricate rings. The "largest 3-ring of the day" (block 49335445: SOL→USDC→cbBTC→SOL, $789) dissolved on inspection: cbBTC→SOL and SOL→USDC were two rows of one user's multi-hop route (same tx 0x0eab3dc5…, KyberSwap hop + a 1inch-LOP hop). Rows must be netted per transaction into user intents first, or routing internals masquerade as matchable demand.
Trap 2 — same-tx opposites are already-captured CoW. The "perfect" WETH/USDC match in block 49325116 ($19.2k vs $19.2k) is one CoW-protocol settlement filling a user order against a 1inch limit order — both legs share tx 0x8ccb7287…. That internalization already happened. The sequencer's incremental opportunity is only cross-transaction matches.

Intent extraction v2 (fixes both — and the trap the v1 fix created)

Trap 3 — found in self-review: uniform per-tx netting mangles batch settlements. A CoW-protocol settlement carries multiple user orders in one tx (3.8% of settlements); per-tx netting merges distinct users into one fake intent — or nets two users' opposite orders into nothing, silently deleting exactly the flow this study cares most about. ERC-4337 bundler txs bundle unrelated users the same way.
  1. Protocol-aware granularity. cow_protocol and uniswap_x rows are order-level by construction (verified: one row per CoW Trade event) — each row is one intent. In those txs, other rows (e.g. a 1inch-LOP fill inside a CoW settlement) are the solver's liquidity legs, not intents. All other (router) flow: net per transaction.
  2. Net in token units, not USD. Hop legs cancel exactly in token amounts; a single NULL-usd_amount row would break USD-based cancellation and mis-net the tx. USD enters only at valuation; txs with unvaluable legs are counted, never silently dropped.
  3. Canonicalize ETH ≡ WETH before matching — wrapping is free to a batcher, so a native-ETH sell matches a WETH buy. Stables are not merged (USDC/USDbC/DAI carry real spread).
  4. Keep txs netting to one sold + one bought token → one intent. Split routes collapse correctly (verified: a 2×$20k dual-aggregator split netted to one $40k intent). Multi-token txs: excluded and counted.
  5. Matching is computed across intents only (cross-tx for router flow; cross-order for batch settlements — two orders already crossed inside one settlement are realized, not potential).
  6. Same-sender guard: matches where both sides share sender_address (MM rebalancing, arb loops) are not user-value CoW — headline excludes them, reported separately.

Hindsight's netting philosophy transplanted: decline ambiguity, count what you drop, never record a wrong intent.

Metrics

Per block (and per multi-block window), over the netted intents:

W = Σ intent USD # addressable volume
pairwise_matched = Σ over pairs 2·min(flow_A→B, flow_B→A)
multilateral_cap = W − ½·Σ over tokens |net_demand_t| # rings & chains included
  • Pairwise matched volume — trade volume internalizable by direct A↔B crossing. Exact, conservative, and the number closest to what a simple in-sequencer matcher could capture.
  • Multilateral cap — what a full batch auction with uniform clearing prices could internalize: only each token's net demand must touch AMMs. Crucially, this bound already contains route-mediated netting (below), because re-routing an order never changes any token's endpoint imbalance.
  • Crossing surplus (pairwise, empirical) — no AMM model needed: both sides' executed prices are in the data. If sellers received p_sell (quote per base) and buyers paid p_buy, crossing at any price between them yields total surplus
    surplus = matched_base_qty × max(0, p_buy − p_sell)
    computed in token units, valued in USD, floored at zero — a negative gap (intra-block price move) means crossing helps no one; the negative-gap share is reported as its own statistic, not silently clipped.
  • Window sweep — every metric at tumbling (non-overlapping) windows of 1, 5, 15, 30, 150 blocks (2 s → 5 min). CoW density grows superlinearly with window; this chart is the report's centerpiece and directly answers "what if the batch waited?"
  • Splits — by pair tier (bluechip / stable / long-tail), by venue/project, by maker-involvement (intents whose tx contains RFQ/LOP maker fills — 1inch_lop, bebop_rfq, native_rfq — matchable liquidity but not retail demand), and by same-sender (excluded from headline).

Decomposing the cap: direct, rings, and route-mediated ("synthetic") CoW

A batch with uniform clearing prices can net more than opposite endpoint pairs. An order's route legs can oppose other orders: with order 1 USDC→WETH and order 2 PEPE→USDC where PEPE/USDC is thin but PEPE/WETH is deep, the batch routes order 2 as PEPE→WETH→USDC — its WETH→USDC leg crosses order 1 peer-to-peer, the thin pool is never touched, and only the deep pool absorbs the residual. This synthetic CoW by route choice is unique to batch settlement, and it is why the multilateral cap — not the pairwise number — is the true ceiling.

The scan decomposes the multilateral cap into three labeled buckets:

multilateral_cap = pairwise_direct + ring_attributable + route_mediated_residual
  1. Pairwise direct — as above, with exact empirical surplus.
  2. Ring-attributable — after removing pairwise-matched flow, greedy cycle cancellation on the residual intent-flow graph (3- and 4-cycles, min-edge cancelling). Ring surplus from the price product around the cycle: ring_qty × max(0, 1 − Π p_leg). Flow decomposition is greedy and non-unique — stated, not hidden.
  3. Route-mediated residual — the remainder: netting achievable only by choosing routes through shared tokens. Its volume is exact (it is a difference of exact quantities); its surplus is valued per netted token-unit by a per-token empirical AMM-cost proxy measured from this same dataset (the distribution of pairwise execution-price gaps per token tier — netted long-tail flow is worth ~100× netted bluechip flow). Labeled proxy, never mixed with exact numbers. The realizable value of this bucket is exactly what the APEX simulation phase prices later — Phase 0 sizes the bucket, APEX prices it.

The full scan

Tooling: Python + Polars, not Rust

Data arrives via ~7 per-day Allium extraction queries (intent-level rows: block, tx, sold, bought, USD, protocol tags — ~105k rows/day, ~13 MB/day). All matching math is dataframe group-bys over ≤1M rows — seconds in Polars. Rust buys nothing here; Python buys iteration speed and the plotting for the report. The script is rerunnable and window-parameterized; if this graduates into a recurring monitor, the logic ports into hindsight later.

Pipeline

  1. Extract — per-day SQL (the netting CTEs above) → intent parquet files, plus excluded-tx counters.
  2. Match — per window (1/5/15/30/150 blocks): pairwise matched volume + exact crossing surplus per pair; multilateral cap per window; maker/venue/pair-tier splits.
  3. Report — self-contained HTML: window-sweep curves, per-pair league table, surplus distribution (p50/p90/max), share of blocks with any match, and the day-0-style headline numbers over the full week.

Known limitations, owned upfront

  • Theoretical cap, not achievable capture. "Everyone participates, no limit prices bind, at one clearing price." Real capture is strictly below; the APEX simulation is what measures the achievable fraction. This analysis bounds whether that simulation is worth building.
  • Aggregator flow only. Direct DEX-router users (no aggregator) are additional matchable demand not counted here — the cap understates "everyone" in that direction. A follow-up can add single-swap direct-router txs from dex.trades.
  • USD netting tolerance. Netting uses Allium's usd_amount (99%+ populated); matching/surplus math is done in token units and only valued in USD, so price-column noise cannot fabricate matches.
  • Maker fills are matchable liquidity, not retail demand. LOP/RFQ-involved intents are tagged and reported as a separate slice, never silently mixed.
  • Volatility inflates dislocation surplus. Example-3-style gaps partly reflect intra-block moves. The per-pair surplus distribution is reported with medians, not just sums, so one VANA doesn't write the headline.
  • Base has no public mempool — settled per-block composition is the actual matching opportunity a sequencer sees; no MEV-reordering correction needed. (This cuts in our favor: the measure is clean.)
  • odos_v2 is stale in Allium since 07-28 (indexer gap, both chains) — a small known undercount, footnoted in the report.
